# Hiring

## Use

Use when the user is the one hiring: writing the job post, deciding who to interview from a stack of resumes, preparing interviews, replying to applicants, drafting the offer, planning the first weeks.

A candidate's own resume belongs to `writing/resume-writing`. Evaluating someone already employed belongs to `people/performance-review`.

The user makes the hiring decision. This Skill ranks evidence and shows trade-offs; it does not choose.

## Domain inputs and blockers

- The role: title, three to five responsibilities, what is required and what is a bonus, location, and the pay range if it will be published.
- For screening: the resumes and the rubric. No score is given before the rubric exists.
- For an offer: title, level, location, pay, start date, reporting line. Pay figures come from the user; a missing one stays a marked placeholder.

Market pay data is not supplied by this Skill. Without a source the user provides or a search done in the session, say that no market comparison was made.

## Job post

- Every requirement can be checked against a resume. "Strong leader" is not a requirement; "has led a team of three or more" is. Vague ones are rewritten or removed.
- A requirement stays in the must-have list only if a strong candidate without it would be rejected. Everything else is a bonus.
- Pay is a number or a range, or the section is left out. "Competitive" says nothing.
- Shape: why the role exists, the company in a few sentences, what success looks like after a year, four to seven responsibilities starting with a verb, requirements split into required and bonus, pay and benefits, how to apply. About 400 to 700 words.
- Plain, inclusive wording: no "rockstar" or "ninja", no gendered terms, no degree requirement unless the job needs it.

## Screening

1. **Build the rubric before reading any resume**, since a rubric written afterwards bends towards whoever looked good. Three tiers: must-have (absence eliminates; keep it to three or four, such as a legal licence or availability), should-have (weights adding up to 100, none above 40), nice-to-have (small weight, never eliminates).
2. Score each should-have item 3 (clear evidence), 2 (adjacent), 1 (thin but not contradicted), 0 (absent or contrary), or mark it "cannot assess". Cannot assess is not zero: the item leaves both numerator and denominator, and the result is reported as a percentage of the weight that could be assessed, with that weight shown.
3. Every score quotes the resume. A score without a quotation is an impression. What the resume does not say is marked cannot assess, never inferred.
4. Remove from view before scoring, and never score: name, age or graduation year, gender, photo, address or commute, school prestige, employer brand, employment gaps, accent, pay history, arrest record, "culture fit". In many places scoring on these is unlawful as well as unfair. Asked to filter on one of them, decline once and offer the lawful version (for example an interview question about a gap instead of a penalty).
5. Sort into interview, maybe, no, and cannot assess. Someone in the last group is missing one fact: a two-line question settles it.

## Interviews

- Four to six competencies per role, each assigned to one interview stage and one interviewer, so no panel asks the same thing twice.
- Per competency, behavioural questions ("tell me about a time…") with two or three follow-ups; situational questions only where the candidate could not have the experience yet. No leading or double questions.
- A scorecard with anchors written for this role at each level of the scale, not generic adjectives.
- Debrief: everyone records a score before discussion, time goes to the disagreements, and an eliminating signal is discussed separately from the total.
- A small company's default loop is a short screening call, a working session on real work, and a final conversation.

## Replies and offer

- Every applicant gets an answer: an invitation with time options, a hold with a date that is then kept, or a decline that is kind, prompt and honest. Two weeks of silence costs more than a clear no.
- A decline gives a factual reason only when it is true and useful, and never touches the list in point 4. Before sending in bulk, list every recipient by name per group.
- The offer shows total first-year pay, not only base: base, bonus target, equity with amount and vesting, signing bonus. It states start date, reporting line, location, employment type, the conditions it depends on and how long it stays open. Notes for the hiring manager on negotiation room go outside the letter.
- First weeks: accounts and equipment confirmed working the day before, a first day about setup and people rather than deep work, a buddy who is not the manager, and check-ins after one week, two weeks and a month against the rubric used to hire.

## Constraints

- Employment terms are jurisdiction-specific. Clauses such as at-will employment or exempt status are US concepts and are replaced, not translated, elsewhere; an offer letter is reviewed by someone who knows the local law before it is sent.
- Whether someone is an employee or a contractor, and how they are paid, is confirmed with an accountant or lawyer, not chosen here.
- No date of birth, home address, identity number or protected characteristic appears in any score, note or output.
- Do not invent a reason for a rejection, a market rate or a benefit the company does not have.
