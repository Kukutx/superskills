# Selling a Business

## Use

Use when the user is the seller: deciding who might buy, writing the one-page teaser and the fuller memorandum, asking buyers for offers, and keeping a sale process on track.

The buyer's side (screening, diligence, returns) belongs to `finance/deal-diligence`. What the business is worth belongs to `finance/company-valuation`. The sale agreement belongs to `legal/contract-review`.

Sale documents make statements a buyer will rely on. They are drafted here and go out only after the owner has checked every fact and a lawyer has reviewed the confidentiality and legal wording.

## Domain inputs and blockers

What the business does and how it earns; three to five years of financials, or as many as exist; growth, margins and what is recurring; the three to five real selling points; what must stay anonymous; the kind of buyer wanted (a strategic owner, a financial investor, whether the owner stays) and the timetable; and any buyers to include or leave out.

Buyers are named from what the user knows or from sources checked in the session. A fund's size, a company's recent acquisitions or its appetite are never supplied from memory.

## Disclose in three layers

Each layer reveals only what its stage needs:

1. **Teaser**, anonymous, to find out who is interested.
2. **Information memorandum**, after a confidentiality agreement is signed, to support a first offer.
3. **Data room**, for the buyers who made the shortlist, to confirm what the memorandum said.

Keep a log of who received what and when; it is the record of the process.

## Teaser

One page: a project code name and industry description; two or three sentences on the company without naming it; four to six highlights (market position, quality of revenue such as recurring share and retention, growth, margins, team, strategic value); a financial summary; and what is on offer (all of it, a majority, growth capital), the timetable and a contact.

Anonymity check: no company, brand or product name; a region, not a city; no named customers or partners; no headcount if it would identify the business; a revenue range rather than an exact figure in a small industry; no logo or screenshot. Give enough financial detail to filter out the merely curious, and use "leading" or "fast-growing" only when it is true.

## Information memorandum

- Sections: summary with five to seven highlights; the company; the industry; growth opportunities; customers and sales; operations; financials; appendix. A large company's memorandum runs to dozens of pages; a small business needs far fewer, and the test is whether a buyer can make a first offer from it.
- The highlights answer the three things every buyer asks: how it grows, how it makes its margin, and what protects it.
- Every claim carries its number: "revenue grew 15% a year from 2021 to 2024", not "strong growth".
- The financial section shows the bridge from reported to adjusted profit with each adjustment explained, working capital and capital spending. Adjusted and pro-forma figures are labelled as such.
- It is a sales document and leads with strengths, but it does not hide a material problem: the buyer will find it in diligence, and the price falls further then.
- The story has to support the asking price, because the memorandum sets the frame for valuation.
- Customer names stay anonymous until late in the process unless the seller agrees otherwise.

## Buyers

| Kind | Why they would buy |
| --- | --- |
| direct competitor | share, or removing a rival; flag any competition-law question |
| adjacent company | a product extension or cross-selling |
| customer or supplier | vertical integration |
| company building a platform | a missing capability |
| investor seeking a platform | a first company in the sector |
| investor with a company already in the sector | an add-on; name which of its companies and the fit |
| growth investor | a stake in a fast-growing business |

- Three tiers, contacted in order: a handful of best fits with a clear reason and a record of buying; a second group; a third used only if the process needs widening. Thirty to forty well-researched buyers beat two hundred names.
- A buyer that just bought in the sector either wants more or has spent its money; work out which. A fund near the end of its investment period may be keener.
- For the top tier, note who decides, whether a relationship exists, known limits on size or structure, and the best way in.

## Asking for offers

A first, non-binding offer should state: the valuation range; the form of payment (cash, shares, deferred or earn-out payments, the seller keeping a stake); where the money comes from and how certain it is; what diligence is still needed; the expected time to completion; conditions; and who the buyer is and why they want it.

A final offer adds: comments on the draft agreement, committed financing, remaining diligence, exclusivity terms, any regulatory approval and its timing, terms for key people, and which parts are binding.

Always say how offers will be judged — price, certainty, speed, fit — since buyers shape their offer to it. Two to three weeks for first offers and three to four for final ones are common allowances.

An invitation to meet management states time, place, attendees, the agenda, the ground rules and how follow-up questions are handled.

## Milestones

Materials drafted, buyer list agreed, teaser out, confidentiality agreements signed, memorandum out, first offers due and reviewed, shortlist, management meetings, data room open, final offers due and reviewed, exclusivity granted, confirmatory diligence, agreement signed, approvals, completion. Each is on track, at risk, delayed or done, with an owner and a date; a slipping milestone is raised early, and buyer feedback is logged, because a pattern in it is a reason to change the approach.

## Output

The document asked for (teaser, memorandum outline or sections, buyer table, offer request, meeting invitation, tracker), with every unverified buyer fact marked, the anonymity check run for a teaser, and the list of facts the owner must confirm.

## Constraints

- No figure, customer, contract or growth claim that the user has not supplied; a gap is a marked placeholder.
- Nothing identifying in a teaser.
- Tax, structure and the legal effect of an offer are for the seller's accountant and lawyer.
- This is preparation, not financial or legal advice, and nothing is sent from here.
