# Offers, Models and Price Research

Read when choosing a charging model, designing a guarantee or bonuses, running price research or reviewing a pricing page in detail.

## Charging models

| Model | Fits when | Watch for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| flat rate | one clear use; start here when it works | the largest customers pay too little |
| usage | value grows with use | bill shock; add caps or alerts |
| tiers | distinct groups with different needs | too many tiers |
| per seat | value grows with the number of people | shared logins when it does not |
| by feature | features separate light from heavy users | locking the feature that is the core value |
| credits | each action has a real cost, as in AI products | unclear worth of a credit |
| by outcome | the outcome can be measured and attributed | disputes about attribution |
| hybrid | a platform fee plus usage | complexity |

Let the value metric choose the main model, and add a second only when one model clearly misprices a kind of customer.

Two tiers suit a clean split between small and large customers; three is the norm; four or more only across a very wide range of customer sizes. Before adding a tier, ask whether it serves a real under-served group or only adds a choice. Tiers differ by feature, usage limit, support level, or access and customization.

**Free plan or trial.** A free plan fits when free users bring value (content, data, referrals), the market is large, serving a user costs little and there is a clear upgrade trigger; the risks are users who never convert and a product that looks cheap. A trial fits when value takes time to show and the price is higher: long enough for the value to appear, with full access rather than a cut-down version. A reverse trial gives full access first and drops to the free plan at the end.

## Strengthening an offer

How to pull each lever:

- **Outcome**: state the result behind the surface request, specifically.
- **Believability**: named cases, a named method, a guarantee, proof from people like the buyer, and what happens when it does not work. A stuck offer usually lacks proof, not features.
- **Time to value**: a first small win within a week, material up front, "do this one step today".
- **Effort**: done-for-you parts, fewer decisions, templates, working asynchronously.

Find the real objections before diagnosing: read the refund requests and sales conversations of the last six months, read the sales page aloud and list every doubt, and ask three recent buyers what nearly stopped them. The answers cluster into a handful, and each gets a bonus or a guarantee. When two levers are equally weak, change the one that is easier to move and unlocks more, then the other. A promised timescale missed once lowers credibility for every later buyer.

**Guarantees.** Choose the type from four questions: what risk the buyer fears most (no result, not on time, paid for nothing, a scam); how many refunds the business can absorb; how experienced the buyer is (a first-time buyer needs a generous refund, an experienced one a service level or payment by result); and whether the result can be measured (a vague result is guaranteed on the condition that the buyer did the work).

- At most two conditions, each specific and checkable.
- Stress test: if one buyer in ten claimed it, could the business pay?
- Place it above the purchase button, not in the small print.
- "Cancel any time" is not a guarantee. A guarantee does not replace proof.
- For software, a free trial or a refund window on an annual plan is the strong form.

**Bonuses** answer objections: no time (the first week done for them), which tools (a vetted list), what if I get stuck (a way to ask), my team will not agree (material for convincing them), I tried this before (a case in the same situation). Three to five, each named and with a price that could be defended; something that would never be sold on its own gets no price. Bonuses sit on top of a complete core; a weak core with strong bonuses produces angry refunds. Audit each: which objection it closes, when the buyer receives it, whether a competitor could offer the same, and whether it would sell without the core, which would mean the core is the weak part.

**Scarcity** comes last, after the outcome, the proof, the speed and the effort, and only from a real limit: capacity, a cohort date, a founding price that really rises, stock, a season, a bonus that is really withdrawn. Not needed for a subscription that can be cancelled any time, a small impulse purchase, a premium brand or an audience that already trusts the seller. If no real limit exists, use none; "last chance" is said once.

## Price research

Four questions per respondent: at what price is it too expensive to consider, so cheap that quality is doubted, getting expensive but still considered, a bargain. Plot the four cumulative curves and read their crossings:

| Crossing | Gives |
| --- | --- |
| "too cheap" with "getting expensive" | the lower bound of the acceptable range |
| "too expensive" with "bargain" | the upper bound |
| "too cheap" with "too expensive" | the optimal price point |
| "getting expensive" with "bargain" | the point of indifference |

A hundred to three hundred responses, split by segment. The method yields a range people accept, not proof that demand survives a price rise. Asking "what would you pay" directly is biased; asking whether they would buy at a stated price, varied across respondents, is better.

To decide what goes in which tier, have respondents pick the most and least important of small sets of features: the most valued belong in every plan, the middle ones separate the tiers, the next go in the top plan, and the least valued are dropped or sold as add-ons. Usage data can also show the level of use beyond which customers stay and expand, which is a natural place for a limit.

## Pricing page review

Rate ten points as pass, partial or gap, then give the fixes ordered by effect against cost and the one to do first. No total score.

- **For the human buyer**: the value stated above the fold in the buyer's words; who each plan is for and how they differ; whether a choice can be made in half a minute (many tiers or unexplained terms are a gap); trust signals beside the button; a recommended plan and consistent price endings; real prices with no hidden overage, not "contact us" on every tier.
- **For a machine reader**: real numbers in the page text, not in an image or loaded only by script; an FAQ covering what it can do, the limits, cancellation and trials; inclusions and limits in words, not only ticks; structured data and a page crawlers are allowed to read.

When every tier hides its price, people and machines turn to a competitor that gives a number; show at least a starting price or a typical range. Gaps on the machine side are often cheap to fix.
