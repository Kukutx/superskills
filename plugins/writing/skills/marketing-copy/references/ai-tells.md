# AI-sounding patterns in detail

Read when copy has to be cleaned of machine-written patterns, or when a draft keeps failing the checks in the entry.

## How to rewrite

- Rewrite from the fact. A synonym swap produces the next tell.
- Do not turn one fix into a template. If every contrast reveal becomes ", because …" and every list of negatives becomes "There's nothing to …", those are the new tics.
- **Stacking test.** Two or more of the patterns below in one paragraph: rewrite the paragraph, do not patch them one by one.
- Keep the brand's register. A formal sentence that makes a specific claim is fine; squeezing a corporate voice into clipped startup lines is its own tell.
- Never invent a number to sound less generic. Replacing "industry-leading" with a made-up figure is worse; write `[NEED: proof]` and ask.
- When the material on hand cannot support a headline that passes the swap test, write the best version and add `[NEED: differentiator vs. named competitors]`.

## The three structural tells

**Trailing add-on.** Once the main clause is complete, anything after the comma that adds no new fact is deleted: an "-ing" clause such as "ensuring nothing slips through the cracks", a list of negatives, a string of repeated benefits. A clause that adds a fact stays ("Imports run nightly, keeping your existing IDs"). End the sentence where the claim ends; if the benefit matters, give it its own sentence with one concrete detail.

**List of negatives.** Rewrite it as what actually happens, in the order it happens. A true "no" that matters is said once, beside the action ("Free to start. No card required."). When the brief contains several, turn the rest into events: "no signup form" becomes "Log in with your Shopify account". A single "without" or a "Never X again" headline is fine, as long as a list of negatives does not follow it.

**Contrast reveal**, including its variants: "Not just X, but Y", "Less X, more Y", "X? Think again", "This isn't X. This is Y.", "The question isn't X, it's Y", "You don't need X. You need Y." To correct a belief the reader holds, state the correct fact and its evidence; do not stage a denial.

## Vocabulary

- **Change on sight**: delve, tapestry, testament to, realm, embark, beacon, multifaceted, paradigm, synergy, myriad, plethora, meticulous, intricate, utilize, leverage as a verb, supercharge, turbocharge, game-changer, "ever-evolving landscape", "plays a crucial role".
- **Two in one paragraph means rewrite the paragraph**: seamless, robust, streamline, empower, elevate, unlock, harness, foster, optimize, cutting-edge, innovative, holistic, comprehensive, pivotal, powerful, world-class, ecosystem, journey; and the empty intensifiers truly, genuinely, incredibly, fundamentally, significantly, simply, actually, literally, quietly.
- The replacement is the fact the word stood for: "robust" becomes "99.99% uptime over two years", when that is true and supplied.
- **Dashes**: none in short copy (headline, subhead, hero, call to action, ad, social post, email subject); at most one or two a page in long copy.

## Openings, closings and stock lines

| Instead of | Write |
| --- | --- |
| "In today's fast-paced world" | the customer's problem |
| "Whether you're a solo founder or a Fortune 500" | who this page is for |
| "Imagine…" | the actual before and after |
| "When it comes to…" | the subject, directly |
| "Here's the thing:", "The truth is", "Let me be clear" | nothing; if the claim cannot stand, add evidence |
| "In this guide we'll explore", "Let's dive in" | the first point |
| "In conclusion", "Ultimately" | the last concrete point or the action |
| a mock-profound closing line ("Because growth shouldn't wait.") | nothing |
| "Moreover", "Additionally", "Importantly" | nothing |
| "Say goodbye to X" | the new routine ("Reconciliation runs nightly. You review the exceptions.") |
| "Take X to the next level" | which measure, to what target |
| "Effortless", "in just a few clicks" | the real number of steps or minutes |
| "Join thousands of happy customers" | the real count, logos or a named quotation |
| "Everything you need", "all-in-one" | the two or three things a buyer actually checks |
| "Built for teams who move fast" | the real customer and trigger ("For subscription teams fighting failed payments") |

## Vagueness, inflation and format

- Weak sourcing ("experts agree", "studies show", "trusted by industry leaders"): name the source or the customer, or delete.
- Inflated importance ("marks a pivotal moment", "redefines the category"): state the fact and let the reader judge.
- Stacked hedges ("can help you potentially reduce"): say what is known, or state the real limit.
- A coined label ("the integration tax") only if the whole site will define it and keep using it.
- Vulnerability with no real story behind it, and chummy openers in emails and posts ("Great question!", "We get it."): remove.
- Over-explaining ("Let's break this down", "Think of it as…"): give the explanation.
- Format tells, by the two-in-a-paragraph rule: emoji as bullets in business copy, a bold label on every bullet, every bullet opening with "Ensures / Provides / Enables", Title Case everywhere, decorative Unicode, headings of the "What / Why / How" kind. Open bullets with the customer's result, vary the sentence shape, and make a heading a claim.

## Plain words

because (not "due to the fact that"); to ("in order to", "for the purpose of"); before ("prior to"); after ("subsequent to"); if ("in the event of"); more than ("in excess of"); soon ("in the near future"); now ("at the present time"); many ("a large number of", "numerous"); use ("utilise"); start ("commence"); buy ("purchase"); help ("assistance"); make sure ("ensure").

Delete outright: a total of, absolutely, basically, at the end of the day, at this moment in time, obviously, of course, quite, the fact of the matter is, last but not least.

Announcing a section's structure belongs in a long guide; a marketing page just says it. A question as a subheading is for a question readers really ask, followed by a real answer. Not every sentence needs a transition. Where a limit is real, say it with "may", "tends to" or "generally".
