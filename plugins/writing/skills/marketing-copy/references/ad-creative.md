# Ad creative

Read when writing a batch of ads, hooks for video ads, a creative plan, or a review of ads that already ran. Budgets, bidding and reading account data belong to `marketing/paid-ads`.

## A concept, and what ranks it

A concept is segment × motivation × angle × format, with evidence. "User-style video for mums" is not a concept.

Rank concepts by the strength of their evidence:

1. an ad in the user's own account that converted with this angle and segment;
2. the customer's own words, recurring in reviews or calls;
3. a competitor's ad that has run for two months or more (assume it works; borrow the angle, not the execution);
4. organic engagement in the niche;
5. a pattern from another category;
6. the team's instinct, which gets a cheap test or is dropped.

Evidence decides the order. Production level is a separate decision: even a well-evidenced concept starts cheap.

## The hook has three parts that must not repeat each other

For video: what happens on screen in the first seconds (stops the thumb), the first spoken line (opens the question), and the on-screen text (anchors the claim for someone watching silently). Write all three; one alone is a third of a hook. If the voice says "I stopped paying $200 a month for my gym", the screen shows the cancellation email and the text names the replacement.

For a static ad the same rule holds for image and headline: the headline is never a caption for the picture.

## Writing hooks

- Work down: segment, then motivation in the customer's exact words (their language beats a marketing paraphrase), then format (street interview, selfie point-of-view, screen recording, unboxing, side-by-side demonstration, text over a still, founder to camera, reaction), then the three parts.
- Deliver a matrix: `# | Segment | Motivation and where the words came from | Format | Screen | Spoken | Text`. Ten hooks across ten segment-and-motivation cells teach more than thirty rewrites of one cell.
- Each opening device has a condition: a curiosity gap is paid off inside the ad, or it is clickbait; a bold claim is proved on screen or in the next beat; a first-person confession needs a lived detail; a point-of-view opener depends entirely on being specific; a question uses the words a buyer would type into search; a countdown or challenge has an ending; proof shown first must speak for itself.
- The beat after the hook continues the hook's premise and does not turn into a product pitch. A new hook therefore needs a new following beat; testing a hook also tests what follows it.

## Diagnosing by stage

| Weak stage | Change |
| --- | --- |
| stopping (people scroll past in the first seconds) | the opening picture |
| holding (they stop, then leave) | the beat after the hook, not the hook |
| clicking | the promise, the action or the proof |
| converting after the click | the landing page does not continue the ad: change the page or the claim |

A high stop rate is not a good ad: clickbait shows as a strong stop with holding and conversion collapsing. Change one component per test.

## Account state

- **Nothing works yet**: go wide, not deep. Mostly new concepts, few iterations; count a win on any single measure that improved; iterate only on what showed a signal. Check for creative that is too safe, a message that is too complicated, an unclear offer, and an audience too narrow to be affordable.
- **Something converts profitably**: go deep on it, but each variant must look clearly different, because a near copy only takes reach from the original and teaches nothing. Add a version in another tone and sub-angles for the winning segment, and keep a small share for exploration: winners tire, and the next winner is rarely an iteration of the current one.

Production levels: iteration in hours (a new hook, copy or crop); a remix in days from existing material; a full production in weeks, only for an angle already proved in the account or by a cheap test. Start cheap, and spend on production only after a cheap version has shown a signal. Plan the number of pieces the team can make well and cut the rest by evidence.

Reviewing: judge the concept, not the single ad (three executions failing means the concept is wrong; one failing means the execution); read the stages, not only the return; draw no conclusion from too small a sample; record every lesson somewhere it changes the next plan, with the review or the ad it came from.

## Formats and their risks

- Some formats reach new, cold audiences; others convert people already considering. Run both, and do not cut a converting format because it cannot open a cold audience.
- A founder's letter or origin story is a dependable first format for a cold audience; a grid of several products is the easiest when there are several. Callouts, an FAQ card, before and after, and a comparison with an alternative mostly convert the middle.
- For each static concept give: format, headline, body, a description of the visual, and where the material comes from.
- Before-and-after claims are regulated in health, finance and beauty. A named comparison must be true and provable, and some platforms restrict naming. A press logo is used only when that outlet really covered the product and its terms allow it. Never fake a social media screenshot. Every claim in an educational graphic is provable.
- An ad dressed as a chat, a notes app or an AI answer is a dramatization: every claim in it must be true of the product, it is not presented as a real customer's message or a conversation someone happened upon, and health, medical or financial advice placed in a fake AI answer needs legal review. Such interfaces may also imitate another company's look; a cautious brand uses a generic chat screen.

## Search ads assembled from parts

Where the platform combines headlines and descriptions freely, a workable mix for fifteen headlines is three or four keyword-led, three or four benefit-led, two or three with social proof, two or three action-led, one or two on the difference and one with the brand name; for four descriptions, benefit with proof, feature with outcome, social proof with the action, and an offer or deadline with the action when one exists. This mix is a starting point, not a platform rule. Pin the brand name only when the brand's rules require it; leave the rest unpinned. Give a keyword-insertion slot a safe default, since the inserted term can exceed the limit. Spaces and symbols count.

Writing for several platforms at once, start from the strictest format and expand, so the core message holds everywhere.

## Showing concepts for approval

Two to four concepts; ten is a menu nobody finishes. Name each frame of a carousel by its job in the story (hook, problem, mechanism, result, context, ask), not by what it shows. Each concept states what in it is real: which test, which review, which footage. Proof that does not exist yet is labelled "illustrative"; invented figures and stock images are never passed off as the brand's own.
