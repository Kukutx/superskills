# Marketing Copy

## Use

Use to write or edit public marketing text: home, landing, pricing, feature and about pages, ad headlines and descriptions, blog posts, newsletters, press releases and case studies. Also use when existing copy "reads like AI", is flabby, or needs a check for brand voice and risky claims before it is published.

App Store and Google Play fields belong to `writing/app-store-copy`. An email to one person belongs to `writing/business-email`; an automated series belongs to `marketing/email-sequences`. Social posts and threads belong to `writing/social-content`. Text inside the product belongs to `writing/ux-copy`. If nobody can say who the page is for or why the product is different, that is `marketing/product-positioning`.

## Domain inputs and blockers

- **The piece and its single action.** What type of content it is and the one thing a reader should do after it. For an ad, also the platform and placement.
- **Audience and their objections**, the difference from alternatives, and the proof on hand (numbers, customer quotes, reviews). For a landing page or ad, where the traffic comes from, because the headline has to match it.
- **Brand voice.** When the user supplies one, apply it and say so. Without one, write in a neutral professional voice; do not invent a brand personality.
- **Missing facts are placeholders.** A proof point, differentiator, result or quotation that was not supplied is written as `[NEED: what is missing]`. Case-study results, customer quotations and the spokesperson quotation in a press release are requested from the user; they are never composed.

## Writing rules

1. Clear beats clever. A specific number replaces "saves time"; the customer's own words from reviews, interviews and tickets replace the company's.
2. One page has one primary action; one section advances one argument.
3. The opening of a page moves from the reader's current discomfort to the better state to the path for getting there.
4. **Swap test.** If a sentence would be just as true on a competitor's site, rewrite it with the fact that makes it this product's, or mark `[NEED: differentiator]`.
5. **Call to action** = verb + what the reader gets + a qualifier when needed ("Start the free trial", "Get the full checklist"). "Submit", "Sign up", "Learn more", "Click here" and "Get started" say nothing. On a landing page the first one is visible without scrolling; in an email it comes after the value; in a blog post it is at the end.
6. Urgency and risk reducers ("no card required", "cancel any time") appear only when they are true.
7. By page type: a home page serves several audiences and starts from the broadest value; a landing page carries one message and one action and its headline echoes the ad or link that brought the reader; a pricing page makes the recommended plan obvious and answers "which one is for me"; a feature page runs feature, then benefit, then outcome; an about page still ends in an action.
8. No exclamation marks. Headlines may be bolder than the brand's usual tone; body text is plainer.

## Formats

Working defaults, not platform limits; the user's style guide or the channel's own rules override them.

| Piece | Shape |
| --- | --- |
| Blog post | title of about 60 characters or fewer containing the main keyword; an opening of 100–150 words; three to five sections, each with one idea and at least one figure, example or quotation; paragraphs of two to four sentences; a close of 75–100 words with the action |
| Newsletter | subject under 50 characters; preview text that adds to the subject; one main action; more "you" than "we"; no opening that introduces the company; one proof point instead of a feature list; never a fake "Re:" or "Fwd:" |
| Landing page | headline under ten words stating the main benefit; subhead; three or four value points; social proof; answers to objections; the action repeated at the end |
| Press release | headline under 80 characters; dateline; a lead of two or three sentences answering who, what, when, where and why; a quotation; company boilerplate; media contact |
| Case study | "[Customer] achieved [result] with [product]"; snapshot box; challenge, solution, measured results, customer quotation, action |

For web pages add a title and meta description suggestion, with one main keyword and two or three secondary ones placed in the title, the first paragraph, one subheading and the URL, and one H1 per page.

**Ads.** Decide three to five angles first (pain, outcome, social proof, curiosity, comparison, urgency, identity, contrarian). Variants differ by angle; changing a word does not make a variant, and three near-identical headlines teach nothing after the money is spent. A description adds proof, answers an objection or strengthens the action; it does not repeat the headline. Where the platform assembles headlines and descriptions in any combination, each line must make sense alone, and the set includes at least one keyword-led, one benefit-led and one action-led headline. Take character limits from the platform's current ad specifications, count every line, and mark any line over the limit together with a trimmed version.

## Editing passes

Run these on existing copy, and on a new draft before delivering it:

1. **So what.** Each claim is followed to its consequence for the reader; a feature gets a "which means" bridge.
2. **Prove it.** "Trusted by thousands", "industry-leading" and "customers love us" need the number, the source or the customer's own words. A claim with no proof is withdrawn or softened, not decorated.
3. **Specific.** What cannot be made specific is usually filler and is cut.
4. **Zero risk.** Look around the action for friction, an unanswered objection, a missing trust signal or a vague "contact us".
5. **Line edit.** Remove very, really, just and actually; "in order to" becomes "to", "utilize" and "leverage" become "use"; passive becomes active; a nominalization goes back to its verb; one idea per sentence, usually no more than 25 words; web paragraphs of two to four sentences.

## AI-sounding patterns

Check every piece, and a batch of ads as a whole. The fix is to go back to the fact and rewrite; swapping in a synonym only produces the next tell.

| Pattern | Looks like |
| --- | --- |
| contrast reveal | "It's not X. It's Y." |
| stacked negatives | "No X, no Y, no Z." |
| trailing add-on | a clause tacked on after a comma that only restates |
| self-answered question and colon reveal | "The result? …", "Here's the thing: …" |
| stock phrases | "Say goodbye to…", "Unlock the power of…", hooks such as "POV:" or "Stop scrolling", an action reading "Get started today" |
| placeholder adjectives | seamless, robust, powerful, streamline, empower, standing in for a fact |
| dashes in short copy | in headlines, descriptions and subject lines |

Limits per section: one sentence fragment and one list of three. A batch varies its sentence shapes. These are not tells and stay: a real question in an FAQ, one true "no card required" beside the action, a list of three when there are exactly three things, and a clause after a comma that adds a new fact.

## Review before publishing

With a brand guide, check voice and tone (quote each sentence that drifts and say why), terminology, agreement with the approved messages, and style rules. Without one, check clarity (the first paragraph states the point), consistency (one name per concept) and correctness. Voice stays fixed while tone moves with the channel and the moment; no voice trait disappears entirely.

Always flag, whatever the guide says: superlatives with no proof or qualifier (best, fastest, only); missing disclaimers on financial, health or guarantee claims; comparative claims a competitor could challenge; regulated-industry wording; testimonials without attribution or disclosure; passages that track someone else's text too closely.

Severity: High breaks the voice, carries a compliance risk or badly weakens the message; Medium departs from the guide without harm; Low is a small style point.

## Output

- **Draft**: the copy by section; two or three alternative headlines and calls to action, each with its reason; meta title and description for a web page; every `[NEED: …]` listed at the end. Ads are grouped by angle with a character count per line.
- **Edit**: the revised copy, then the changes grouped by pass, so the author can accept or reject them.
- **Review**: a two-or-three sentence summary; a findings table (issue, location, severity, suggestion); before and after for the three to five most serious; the claim flags as a separate list with the suggested action.

## Constraints

- Do not invent statistics, testimonials, awards, customer names or results. Invented proof damages trust and creates legal exposure for the user.
- Claim flags are prompts for a legal or compliance review; this Skill does not decide whether a claim is lawful.
- Do not state an ad or channel character limit from memory; an exceeded limit silently truncates the message.
- Do not change the meaning of pricing, guarantee or legal wording to make it read better; mark it for its owner.
