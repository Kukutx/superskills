# Carousels

Read when the deliverable is a carousel or a multi-image post.

## Choose the frame before writing a slide

| The content is | Frame | Slides |
| --- | --- | --- |
| a list of tools, resources or tips | Value stack | 4–14 |
| a personal result with a system behind it | Problem then proof | 6–10 |
| several named techniques on one subject | Tip list | 6–10 |
| a strong opinion on a common practice | Pointed opinion | 4–8 |
| a product or process that can be shown | Demo walkthrough | 5–11 |

Forcing content into the wrong frame, such as a list squeezed into an opinion piece, is a common reason a carousel fails. What needs fixing is usually slide one or the choice of frame, not the middle slides. The slide ranges are working ranges; a platform's current maximum comes from the platform.

## What each slide does

**Value stack.** Cover: the exact number and the exact thing delivered ("[N] [resources] for [role or result]"). Each middle slide: one item or one category with three to six concrete sub-items, same template, no filler. Last slide: one action. The number is a promise that can be checked, so deliver exactly that many; eight tight slides beat fourteen padded ones.

**Problem then proof.** Slide 1 states the result as a fact with its number, not as advice. Slide 2 redefines the real problem. Slides 3–4 give the mechanism, naming tools and steps. The middle carries the literal prompt, template or step-by-step detail, which is what people save. The last slide shows the real output as a receipt and closes what slide 1 opened. It fails when the mechanism is vague ("optimize your workflow"), because the proof then reads as luck.

**Tip list.** Cover: a figure or claim implying most people get this wrong. Slide 2: why the usual way fails, ideally with an analogy. One tip per slide, shown as weak against strong. Last slide: the one sentence that ties the tips together, and an action. Give every tip a name; "the three-second rule" travels, "keep it short" does not. It fails when several tips are one idea restated: merge them and shorten.

**Pointed opinion.** Slide 1: the contested claim. The middle escalates with concrete, sensory detail, not abstract complaint. The slide before last is the fair turn ("I use X every day. My problem is Y."), which shows the objection is to a practice and not to people or a tool. Last slide: a firm, signed close. The fair turn decides whether it reads as conviction or as grievance, and it answers the most likely hostile comment in advance. It fails when the turn is skipped, or when the author's own product visibly does the thing attacked.

**Demo walkthrough.** Slide 1: the product and the finished result. Slide 2: the pain it replaces. Slide 3: the numbered overview ("[N] steps to [outcome] in [time]"), because a reader who knows the length finishes. Middle: one real screenshot per step with a short note. Last: the finished piece and one line of positioning. Use real screenshots, cropped to the action with the control enlarged and one arrow, readable at thumbnail size.

## Rules for every carousel

- One visual template throughout; only the content changes.
- Slide 1 competes alone in the feed as a thumbnail and is written as a hook that stands by itself.
- One idea per slide. Anything needing two sentences of setup is split.
- One action on the last slide, not three.
- The caption is a second hook, not a repeat of slide 1. A document post uploaded as a PDF still needs a caption that works alone; "see attached" is not one.
- On a platform where image posts move faster, shrink each frame towards its minimum.
- Facts and figures are real and the user's own; no borrowed screenshots.
- Judge a carousel by saves and completion rather than likes; with per-slide data, the slide where people leave is the one to fix.
