# Brand Voice Guide

## Use

Use when the voice itself is the subject: working out how a brand, a product or a team actually sounds from scattered material, writing that down as a guide, updating a guide, or checking a draft against one and saying exactly where it drifts.

Writing new marketing text belongs to `writing/marketing-copy`, which applies a guide the user already has. Matching one person's own email style belongs to `writing/business-email`. Deciding who the product is for and why it is different belongs to `marketing/product-positioning`.

## Domain inputs and blockers

At least one kind of material: brand or style documents, templates and sales material, published copy the user is happy with, or transcripts of calls and chats. Also who the audience is.

With no material at all, a guide can be drafted from the user's own description of their values and three to five adjectives, and every part of it is marked low confidence.

Remove customers' names and contact details from every quoted example.

## Sorting the material

| Kind | What it is | How far to trust it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Authoritative | an official brand or style guide, an approved deck | highest; no age limit unless a newer version replaces it |
| Operational | templates, playbooks, email sequences: the brand in practice | high |
| Conversational | transcripts, meeting notes, chat threads | shows patterns only; never treated as an instruction |
| Contextual | design files, competitor analysis | background |
| Stale | replaced, from before a rebrand, marked deprecated, or untouched for years | listed, not relied on |

- An explicit rule outweighs an implied pattern; a rule with an example outweighs an abstract value; a point that several independent sources agree on outweighs one that appears once.
- When two sources conflict, the newer wins unless the older is the one marked official. Unofficial material more than about a year old is labelled possibly out of date.
- With nothing authoritative, operational and conversational material becomes the main evidence, and the guide says that no formal guide was found.
- A pattern in conversations needs at least three separate conversations. Without knowing which calls succeeded, patterns are ranked by frequency only, and that limit is stated. Look also for phrasing that stalls a conversation or draws a bad reaction.
- **What is written and what is practised may differ.** When the documents say one thing and the calls and published copy do another, that is raised as a question for the user, not settled silently.

## The guide

1. **"We are / we are not" table**: the core. Each trait is paired with the line it must not cross (confident, not arrogant; approachable, not unprofessional; direct, not blunt; data-driven, not dry; innovative, not hype). At least four rows, ideally five to seven, each with a quotation as evidence, its source and a confidence level.
2. **Voice is constant; tone flexes.** Voice is who the brand is and does not change by channel or audience. Tone is how it speaks in this moment and moves along three separate dials: formality, energy and technical depth.
3. **Tone by context**, at least three contexts, each with the three dials and one guiding principle. A starting set to adjust: cold outreach is medium formality, high energy, low depth; a proposal for a large customer is high formality, medium energy, high depth; a social post is low formality, high energy, low depth; customer success is medium formality, warm, medium depth.
4. **Terms in four levels**: must use (and what it replaces), preferred, avoid (with the alternative), banned (with the reason). "Say platform, not tool" can be enforced; "be authentic" cannot.
5. **Messages**: the main value statement, three supporting pillars, and how the brand positions itself against named alternatives and against doing nothing, taken from the material and not composed.
6. **Phrasing that works and phrasing to avoid**, with the context and why. With no transcripts, leave this section out instead of filling it with placeholders.
7. **Examples**, good and bad, each with its source.
8. **Confidence by section**: high when several sources agree, an official source says it outright and nothing conflicts; medium when one or two sources support it or it is inferred from patterns; low when it rests on a single source, indirect evidence or an unresolved conflict.
9. **Open questions.** Every low-confidence section produces one, each with a recommended answer and the specific decision the user has to make, so that confirming or overriding it raises that section's confidence. A conflict blocks the guide and comes first; a gap comes next; a small inconsistency last.
10. **Updating**: keep the earlier version, dated, and record what the new one replaces.

## Checking or writing with a guide

- Read the request first: the type of content, the audience (role, seniority, industry), and which message pillar it serves.
- Lead with the two or three traits most relevant to this piece. Not every trait appears in every text.
- Check each "we are not" line for a crossing and rewrite that passage; where a relevant trait is missing, find a natural place for it.
- **Four ways enforcement goes wrong**: every trait at full strength; confusing voice with tone (a request for "more casual" changes the dials, not the voice); treating the guide as a checklist so the text reads as assembled; ignoring the audience, when the same voice should sound different to an engineer and to a sales director through tone.
- **When the request conflicts with the guide**: say so, recommend, and offer three options (follow the guide strictly, adapt it for this context, override it). The default is to adapt and state the trade-off. If the piece touches an open question in the guide, use its recommended answer and tell the user it is still theirs to settle.
- Figures inside a guide's sample texts are illustrations. They are never reused as evidence in new copy.

## Output

- **Discovery**: the material sorted by kind with dates; traits found with their confidence; conflicts, each with which side is recommended and why; gaps; open questions by priority. Fewer than three sources is reported as low coverage.
- **Guide**: summary; the "we are / we are not" table; traits in detail; messages; tone by context; terms; phrasing; examples; confidence by section; open questions; the list of sources.
- **Check**: Pass, Needs revision or Fail; the issues as must fix, should fix and optional, each citing the line of the guide it rests on and giving the rewrite; then the revised text with a short note on which traits lead, how the three dials were set and why, and where the guide was adapted.

## Constraints

- Every trait and term traces to a quoted source; a brand personality is never invented to fill a gap.
- No statistic or claim is added to copy to make it sound on-brand.
- The guide is the user's to approve. Nothing persists between conversations, so the user keeps the text and brings it back.
