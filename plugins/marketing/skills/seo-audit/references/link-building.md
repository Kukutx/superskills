# Link Building

Rules for earning links and third-party mentions, adapted and rewritten from `coreyhaines31/marketingskills` link-building (MIT).

`marketing/seo-audit` diagnoses whether a page's problem is authority or the page itself; this file is the work that follows when links are the bottleneck.

## Two outcomes

Every placement is judged on two outcomes: SEO (does it pass authority to a page that needs it to rank) and AI citations (is it a page AI engines cite for the category's prompts). A mention on a cited page helps even without a link, because shortlists are built from what third-party pages say.

## Step 1: confirm links are the bottleneck

Links fix an authority gap. They do not fix a page that does not match intent, is not indexed, or is thinner than what ranks. Before any outreach:

1. Check the page itself first: indexed, matches the query's intent, covers what the top results cover. If not, fix the page first.
2. Run a page-level link gap: compare referring domains to the target page against the three to five pages that outrank it. Domain-level comparisons hide the real gap, because a strong domain with an unlinked page still loses.
3. Read the gap honestly: competing pages with several times your referring domains from relevant sites means an authority gap; similar link counts with them still winning means a content, intent or freshness gap; them ranking with few links means a low-competition query; few links anywhere on the domain means a foundation gap, starting with directories, partners and reclamation.

Point internal links from the strongest pages to the target page before chasing external ones. It is free and often half the gap.

## Step 2: have something worth linking to

Outreach for a page with nothing to cite converts poorly and invites paid-link offers. Linkable assets, in order of how often they earn links on their own: statistics and data roundups, free tools and calculators, glossary and definition pages, templates, checklists and examples, integration and partner pages. Put the asset near the target page and link to the target page from it.

## Step 3: pick tactics by effort and quality

Start at the top; the first four are the highest-yield work for most sites and need no new content.

| Tactic | Effort | Typical quality |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Unlinked mention reclamation | Low | High |
| Partner, integration, customer and vendor pages | Low | High, relevant |
| Moved, broken and redirected links | Low | Varies |
| "Best X" list inclusion | Medium | High for AI citations |
| Resource and tools pages | Medium | Medium |
| Broken link replacement | Medium | Medium |
| Expert contributions and guest articles | Medium–high | Varies widely; editorial sites only |
| Podcast guesting | Medium | Medium, plus AI citations from transcripts |
| Data-led digital PR and journalist requests | High | Highest ceiling |
| Directories and launch sites | Low | Foundation only |
| Review platforms | Medium | High for AI shortlists |
| Communities | Ongoing | Mentions, rarely links |

For AI citations, the best target is a page an engine already cites when someone asks the category's question. Run the user's prompts and list the third-party pages that show up; those pages, usually "best X" lists, comparison articles, review pages and community threads, are the top of the prospect list whether or not they would give a followed link.

## Step 4: prospect, qualify, reach out

Qualify each prospect before writing anything: relevant (would this site's readers plausibly need the product; topic relevance matters more than domain rating), real (organic traffic that is not collapsing, an engaged audience, editorial standards), reachable (a named person who owns the page), and worth it (would the link go to a page that needs it, or the mention somewhere engines cite).

Outreach is short, specific, and about their page: what was noticed, why the resource helps their readers, one ask. Draft outreach for the user to approve and send; never send it without explicit approval of the list and the copy.

## Step 5: know the rules and the risk

Google's spam policies treat links "primarily for the purpose of manipulating search rankings" as link spam. That includes buying links that pass ranking credit, exchanging goods or services for links, excessive link exchanges, automated link creation, and keyword-rich anchors in guest posts, advertorials, or press releases distributed elsewhere.

**Paid links are inside the policy when they carry `rel="sponsored"` or `rel="nofollow"`.** A followed link that was paid for is outside Google's policy. The likely outcomes are that Google ignores the link or, for a large or obvious pattern, issues a manual action. Products in exchange for a review-with-link count as payment, and the link needs a qualifying attribute. Sponsored listings, newsletter placements and paid "best X" inclusions also need a disclosure; they can still be worth it for AI citations and referral traffic. Link swaps are fine in moderation and when relevant; partner pages built only to cross-link are not. Guest posts are for reach and expertise, with branded or plain anchors in author bios and body links.

Whether to buy placements is the user's decision. Say what is paid and what the rule is, once, so the user decides with the facts; after the user has chosen, do not repeat the warning on every message.

If the user buys followed placements, judge each site on its own: topic fit, real organic traffic, and what else it links out to. Favour a few relevant placements over volume, keep anchors branded or plain, and never promise a ranking lift. Rented links stop working when the payment stops. Sitewide or footer link packages and exact-match anchors are the easiest patterns to detect. Do not use private blog networks or automated link creation.

## Step 6: anchors and pace

Let other people choose the anchor text; natural profiles are mostly branded, URL and generic anchors. Do not plan a link velocity: there is no safe or unsafe number of links per month for earned links, and a sudden spike of low-quality links is a symptom of buying them. Spread links across pages that need them; concentrating every link on one commercial page with matching anchors is the pattern spam reviews look for.

## Step 7: measure both outcomes

| Metric | Cadence |
| --- | --- |
| New referring domains to target pages | Monthly |
| Rankings for target queries (expect lag of weeks to months) | Monthly |
| Mentions on AI-cited pages | Monthly |
| AI mention and recommendation rate for the target prompts | Monthly |
| Outreach funnel: prospects qualified, contacted, replied, placed | Weekly |
| Referral traffic and conversions, by referring domain | Monthly |

Report placements by target page. A total link count hides where the links went: ten links to the blog do not help a pricing page that is losing.

Link building works as a weekly habit more than a campaign: find new targets, qualify them, draft outreach for approval, and track who has already been contacted.
