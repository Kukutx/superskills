# Market Sizing

Read when the question is whether a market is large enough or attractive enough to plan for. An ordinary campaign brief does not need it.

## Define before estimating

State the market in one sentence: geography, buyer, use case and pricing model, and what is excluded. Three layers:

- **Total market**: everyone who could ever buy. It serves a fundraising story and proves no demand.
- **Serviceable market**: the part the product and its channels can actually address.
- **Obtainable market**: what can realistically be won in one to three years. This is the number a plan is built on.

## Estimate at least two ways

| Method | Calculation | Best for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| bottom-up | reachable buyers × annual spend × realistic share | most plans |
| search-led | volume of high-intent queries × share of clicks × visit-to-trial × trial-to-paid | a floor for the obtainable market, since it covers one channel only |
| competitor-led | visible competitors' revenue, added up | checking the serviceable market |
| channel-led | what the current budget and people can reach | early stage |

Compare like with like. Where the results overlap is the working range. Results more than about three times apart mean one assumption is wrong: find it before showing any number.

- Bottom-up far above channel-led: the demand exists, but the budget or the channels cannot reach it. That is a funding question.
- Channel-led far above bottom-up: reachable buyers are undercounted or the channel assumption is optimistic.
- Competitors' combined revenue above the serviceable estimate: the market was drawn too narrowly, or they serve segments that were excluded.

## Presenting an estimate

Never a single unsourced figure. Give the range, the assumptions kept apart from the observed facts, the methods used and whether they agree, a confidence label, and the one assumption that would change the conclusion together with how to test it.

| Confidence | Basis |
| --- | --- |
| high | two or more independent lines of evidence agree, or first-party data exists |
| medium | credible but incomplete |
| low | has a basis, but a thin one |
| unknown | missing or contradictory; becomes a validation task |

Evidence, strongest first: the user's own commercial data; observed market behaviour (competitors' ads, pricing pages, review counts, hiring); search and demand proxies; third-party reports; expert opinion; weak proxies such as an unsourced market figure or a trend article.

Write "serviceable market of roughly 35 to 50 million a year (medium confidence), from seat counts and competitor revenue; the largest unknown is seats per account, and ten customer calls would narrow it", not "the market is 4.27 billion".

Common mistakes: treating the total market as proof of demand; using a global report for a local decision; treating search volume as market size; giving one method's result; forgetting that spreadsheets, outsourcing and doing nothing compete for the same budget.

## Attractiveness scorecard

Score ten factors from 1 to 5: clarity of demand, available budget, weakness of competition, reachability, channel viability, urgency, availability of proof, execution risk (5 is low risk), speed of signal (5 is a signal within two weeks, 1 is months), and strategic fit.

| Total of 50 | Plan |
| --- | --- |
| 40–50 | enter now and plan against the full obtainable market |
| 30–39 | enter narrowly: choose one wedge and size only that |
| 20–29 | validate first: the first 90 days are a validation sprint with a kill condition |
| below 20 | do not enter yet; list it as an open question unless the team has an unusual advantage |

The bands are a working heuristic. A large obtainable market for a small, rare problem still limits what can be paid to acquire a customer.
