# Content Strategy

## Use

Use when the question is what to publish, in what order and on which channel: blog topics, a content plan for the next month or quarter, topic clusters, which products a shop should push in its content, or how to start and grow a community around a product.

Writing the pieces belongs to `writing/marketing-copy` (pages, articles, ads) and `writing/social-content` (posts, scripts, the posting calendar). Auditing a site that does not rank belongs to `marketing/seo-audit`. When the audience or the differentiated value is still undecided, `marketing/product-positioning` comes first, because a topic list built on an unsettled position has to be redone.

## Domain inputs and blockers

- **What the content is for**: search traffic, sales of specific products, sign-ups, or a community. One primary goal; it decides the scoring below.
- **What customers actually ask and object to**: pasted call notes, support questions, survey answers, reviews, forum threads. Without any of these, build the topic list from the product, the audience and search phrasing, and label it as hypotheses.
- **What already exists and how it did**, if the user can paste it.
- **How much the user can produce.** A plan one person cannot keep up is the common failure; size the calendar to the stated hours.
- **For a shop**: a sales export (product, date, revenue, quantity), stock levels and what the owner knows about seasonality.

Search volume, rankings and backlink counts need a paid tool. From pasted keyword exports they can be used; without them, say that demand was not measured and rank topics on the other criteria.

## Pillars and topics

1. **Every piece is searchable, shareable or both, and searchable comes first** because search traffic keeps arriving after the week of publication. Searchable means aimed at one query and matching what the searcher wants; shareable means a new insight, original data or a position people argue with.
2. **Three to five pillars**, each with a reason and a cluster of subtopics under it. Derive them from four directions: what the product does, what the audience struggles with, what people search for, and what competitors cover or leave out. Most content sits in one blog; a dedicated hub structure is worth building only for the main themes.
3. **Tag each topic with its buyer stage**, read from the phrasing of the query:

| Stage | Typical phrasing |
| --- | --- |
| Awareness | what is, how to |
| Consideration | best, versus, alternatives |
| Decision | pricing, reviews |
| Implementation | templates, tutorial |

4. **Where topics come from**, strongest first: customer conversations (a question becomes an FAQ piece, an objection becomes an article, the customer's own wording becomes the headline), survey answers (a theme named by about 30% or more of respondents is high priority, as a working default), forum and community threads, gaps in competitors' content, and what support or sales keep being asked.
5. **Plan distribution while planning the piece**: subheadings that can stand alone as a post, a quotable line, a figure. One interview can yield a transcript, quote graphics, short clips and a written recap. A working checklist per flagship piece: three to five standalone short posts, one thread or carousel, short clips when the source is video or audio, one or two quote or data images, one email to the owned list, and a re-posting schedule, because posting once wastes the piece.
6. **Pace by how long a channel keeps showing a piece.** A short post is gone within hours, a feed post within about a day, a short video can resurface for weeks, a long video or a search-ranked article works for months or years. Short-lived channels reward frequency and repetition; long-lived ones reward depth and evergreen angles.
7. **Close the loop**: questions, objections and reactions to published pieces choose the next flagship, and the best-performing fragments decide what gets made more. Posting on every platform with no flagship and no reuse is the opposite failure.
8. **Owned, rented, borrowed.** Owned channels (email list, blog, own community) are the only asset that stays; they convert and retain. Borrowed ones (podcasts, guest pieces, partners, press) bring discovery, and rented ones (social platforms, ads, marketplaces) bring reach and engagement; both are pointed back to an owned channel and are never the destination. A plan that puts nearly all effort into channels the user does not own is flagged.
9. **A large archive with flat traffic** is audited before anything new is written: each piece is marked update, merge or retire, then regrouped under the pillars to show where a hub with supporting pieces is missing.
10. After scoring, check the spread across buyer stages; a shortlist that is all awareness has no path to a sale. For developers, depth, accuracy and usefulness count and marketing language does not: tutorials, reference-style guides, use cases, templates and data-backed benchmarks.

## Prioritising and the calendar

Score each topic from 1 to 10 on four criteria and sort by the weighted sum. The weights are a working default from one practitioner, not a standard; change them when the goal differs.

| Criterion | Default weight |
| --- | --- |
| Impact on customers | 40% |
| Fit between the content and the market | 30% |
| Search potential | 20% |
| Resources needed (easier scores higher) | 10% |

A starting mix for the calendar is about 60% searchable, 30% shareable and 10% experiments; adjust it after the first results. Per piece, budget for several headline options and editing passes before counting how many pieces fit the period.

## Planning from sales data

For a shop asking what to promote this month:

1. **Ask how "top performer" is measured before ranking**: revenue, margin, speed of sale or a mix. Revenue alone hides a high-margin, low-volume product; compute profit per unit from the user's own margins and compare the computed figures.
2. Use the last 90 days. Sort products into top three to five, bottom three to five, rising over the last 30 days and falling.
3. **Seasonal or one-off?** A good month can be the season, not the marketing. Decide which before recommending more of the same. The owner's own knowledge of their seasons outranks any industry pattern; state the assumed seasonality and ask whether it matches.
4. **Check stock before recommending a push.** A product down to its last handful of units stays off the push list.
5. A product with flat one-off sales and a growing subscriber base is promoted as a subscription, not as a single purchase.

The brief has six short parts: summary, push hard (two or three products with a content angle each), hold steady, reposition or pause (discount, bundle, stop), seasonal opportunities, recommended offers.

## Community

- Build the community around **an identity the members share**, not around the product, and write that identity down in one sentence.
- **Starting from zero**: invite 20 to 50 founding members personally and stay private until there is baseline activity; seed 5 to 10 example posts before opening; answer every post in the early weeks; define the one action members should take each week.
- **Growing**: first find the step where members drop out (join and never post, post once and vanish). Then fix the newcomer path (pinned welcome, introductions, a "start here"), show member results publicly, and keep fixed rituals such as a weekly topic or a monthly question session.
- **Ambassadors** are recruited from people who already recommend the product unprompted, invited one by one, given something worth having and materials to use, and tracked by the traffic and sign-ups they bring.
- **Platform** follows the members and the goal: a chat platform built around communities for developers, players and creators who want real-time talk; a workplace chat tool for business audiences, at the price of feeling like work; a forum for discussion that should stay searchable. Ask who the ideal member is before choosing, and take each platform's current limits from its own pages.
- **Model by goal**: support-led (many repeated questions, peers answer faster than staff; the goal is fewer tickets), product-led (opinionated technical users whose feedback shapes the roadmap), education-led (a learning curve decides activation and retention), founder-led (early stage, the founder's voice is the brand). Most start founder-led and move to one of the others as they grow.
- **The organizer's role changes with size**: at first an architect who knows every member by name and sets the culture; then a manager who builds rituals, moderation and the newcomer path so activity no longer depends on touching every post; then an enabler of ambassadors and sub-groups. Running a large community like a small one, or the reverse, is the common failure.
- A support-led community turns frequent questions into a searchable knowledge base, recognizes members who help, and says publicly when feedback changed the product. Peer help is valued as help, not used as the company's way out of supporting customers.
- A health check ends in an honest verdict: revive with a few rituals, restart around a different identity, or let it go.
- **Health** is read from the share of members active in a month who are active on a given day (about 20% is a working threshold for healthy), the share of newcomers who post within seven days, the reply rate, and the share of posts written by non-staff. Warning signs: most posts come from the team, questions sit unanswered for a day, the same five people write most of the content, newcomers go silent after introducing themselves.

## AI search

- Write for people and organise for clarity; do not produce a separate version "for AI".
- An AI assistant's answer varies from run to run. To measure visibility, take 10 to 20 questions that matter commercially, ask each three to five times on each assistant, and record a ratio such as "cited in 3 of 5"; one run is an anecdote.
- Whether to admit AI crawlers is decided separately for search and discovery, retrieval triggered by a user, and model training, because blocking training is not the same as blocking citation. Take the current crawler names from each vendor's documentation.

## Output

- **Content plan**: the pillars with their reasons and subtopic clusters; a prioritised topic list (working title, searchable or shareable, format, target query and buyer stage, score, and the evidence it came from); the calendar mix for the period sized to the stated capacity; what was not measured.
- **Shop brief**: the six parts above in 200 to 400 words, with the metric used for ranking stated at the top.
- **Community plan**: platform and why, the shared identity, the weekly core action, the newcomer path, rituals, the first 90 days, and the health figures to watch.

## Constraints

- Do not present a search volume, ranking or traffic figure that was not supplied; estimated demand is labelled as an estimate.
- Do not present the scoring weights, the calendar mix or the community threshold as industry standards; they are starting points the user's results replace.
- Do not recommend content that invents customer quotes, statistics or awards; a topic that needs proof the user does not have is listed with the proof it needs.
- Keep to the plan: the pieces themselves are written only when the user asks for them.
