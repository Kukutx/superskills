# Event Marketing

## Use

Use when an event is on the table: "should we sponsor this conference", a booth or a trade show to prepare, a talk to pitch, a webinar to run, a small dinner or meetup to host, or last quarter's events to judge.

The announcement of a product belongs to `marketing/product-launch`. The wider channel plan belongs to `marketing/marketing-plan`. The emails around an event can be drafted here in outline; a full automated series belongs to `marketing/email-sequences`.

## Domain inputs and blockers

- Who the buyer is and how they buy. Events earn their cost where trust and consideration are high: several decision-makers, regulated or conservative buyers, heavily customized offers, sales cycles of half a year or more. For other audiences, digital channels come first and an event is a small experiment.
- For a sponsorship: the price, what is included, and the attendee breakdown by role and company, not the headline attendance.
- What the same money would buy in the user's best existing channel, since that is the comparison, not zero.
- Fees, travel, staff time and build costs come from the user or the organizer's current offer; none is supplied from memory.

## Go or no-go on a sponsorship

1. **Count the ideal customers in the room, as a number.** An audience of 5,000 with 200 of them in it is a 200-person event.
2. Estimate the qualified meetings that number can realistically produce.
3. **Cost per qualified meeting** = (fee + travel + staff time + booth build) ÷ expected qualified meetings. Compare it with what a meeting costs through outbound or ads.
4. A premium over the alternative is justified only when these buyers cannot be reached elsewhere, when absence would be conspicuous, or when the event gives access to executives that money cannot otherwise buy.
5. Warning signs in the offer: attendance given without its composition, impressions as the headline benefit, badge scans counted as leads, last year's sponsors not returning.

A first appearance is judged with a discount: a promising event that loses money in year one is a reason to redesign the presence, not necessarily to drop it.

## Shape of the presence

- Negotiate in this order: a speaking or panel slot (worth more than a larger booth), the right to host a side event, attendee data written into the contract, booth position and hours, and price last.
- **A small hosted dinner or breakfast** for eight to fourteen chosen guests, with personal invitations sent two to four weeks ahead and no pitch, often produces deeper conversations with the accounts that matter than a booth does, for less.
- A trade show is won in the weeks before it: meetings booked in advance, not walk-up traffic. Plan months of preparation for the days on site.
- At the booth: a thirty-second qualifying question, and a context note on each conversation instead of a bare scan. Whoever had the conversation writes the follow-up.
- Events are judged one by one, not as a channel. A few usually produce most of the attributed pipeline; find them, put more into those (a talk, a larger side event, better-suited staff), cut the long tail, and re-rank yearly. Large conferences dilute the audience; regional events of one or two hundred people often return more.

## Webinars

- A problem-led topic, not a product topic; the title is the advertisement. Decide the single next step (trial, demo, audit, purchase) before writing the content. One theme, one promise, one action.
- Structure: say at the start that an offer comes at the end, three points each with evidence, a scripted one-minute transition, one offer, and a few seeded questions.
- Reminders: on registration with a calendar file, a day before with something useful, an hour before, and five minutes before with the link.
- Diagnose by stage: low registration is the topic, title or promise, or the traffic; low attendance is the gap between registration and the event or a missing calendar add; people leaving early means the content was not front-loaded or did not match the promise, so find the minute they left; staying but not converting is the transition or the offer.

## Speaking

A proposal is about what the audience takes away, not about the company: the problem, two or three concrete takeaways and why the speaker is qualified, in about 150 words. Before writing the talk fix four things: who it is for, what they can do on Monday, what the speaker wants from it, and a point of view with evidence for each block. The recording is the lasting asset: confirm the right to republish it, publish it with a text version, and reply to everyone who asked a question within a day or two.

## Follow-up and measurement

Follow up by behaviour, not in one batch:

| After a webinar | After a trade show |
| --- | --- |
| engaged attendees: a personal note within 24 hours that refers to their question | hot (a real conversation and an agreed next step): personal email the same or next day |
| left early: the recording with timestamps and a softer ask | warm (a conversation, no commitment): a personal note with one relevant resource |
| did not attend: the recording with an expiry date, in two or three emails | scan only: one light touch or nothing; never the full sales sequence |

Badge scans and booth traffic are activity, not results. Tag every contact with the event as its source and follow them to qualified conversations, meetings, opportunities and closed business, over a window that matches the sales cycle. Compare cost per qualified meeting and per opportunity with the other channels; that comparison decides next year. Ask new customers where they met the company, since much event influence never shows in tracking.

After each event write a short review against its goal: what worked and what did not, with correlation kept apart from cause, the reusable assets, and what changes in the next plan.

## Output

- **Sponsorship decision**: the audience count, expected meetings, cost per qualified meeting against the alternative, the verdict, and what to negotiate if it is a yes.
- **Event plan**: goal, format, timeline working back from the date, pre-booked meetings target, staffing, follow-up tiers and what will be measured.
- **Webinar plan**: title options, the single next step, outline, reminder schedule, follow-up by tier.
- **Review**: results against goal per event, ranked, with keep, change or cut.

## Constraints

- Do not quote attendance, pricing or a conversion range for an event that the user or the organizer did not supply; published ranges are directional at best.
- Do not count scans, impressions or booth visits as leads.
- Contact details collected at an event are used within the consent given there; adding every scan to a marketing list may breach privacy law, which the user checks for their market.
- Sponsorship contracts are commitments; cancellation and data terms are read before signing.
