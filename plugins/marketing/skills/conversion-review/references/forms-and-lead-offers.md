# Forms and Lead Offers

Read when the job is the detail of a form, a lead magnet or a free tool. A page review without a form problem does not need it.

## Fields

- Name: one field by default; split first and last only when personalization really needs it.
- Phone: optional where possible; when required, say why, format it automatically and handle country codes.
- Company: autocomplete, enrich after submission, or infer from the email domain.
- Role: a dropdown when the category matters, free text when titles vary widely; often optional.
- Message: optional.
- Choices: fewer than five options are radio buttons, not a dropdown; long lists are searchable and end with "other" plus a text field.

Order from easy to hard, with sensitive fields (phone, company size) last. One column by default; only short related fields such as first and last name sit side by side. A long form asks in stages: email, then name and company, then qualifying questions, then contact preference. Ask which fields are actually used afterwards and delete the rest.

## Button and states

The button names the action and what the visitor gets ("Get my quote", "Download the guide"), sits directly under the last field and is aligned with the fields. After the click, all three states exist: loading (button disabled, progress shown), success (what happens next), error (a clear message, focus on the first field in error, a summary when several are wrong, and everything typed is kept).

Near the form: a privacy line, security marks when sensitive data is collected, a testimonial, the expected response time, and how long the form takes.

## Forms by purpose

| Form | Minimum | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| contact | email or name, and a message | phone optional; state the response time and another way to reach the business |
| demo request | name, email, company | phone optional with a preferred contact method; one question about the use case; an embedded calendar raises attendance |
| quote | staged, easy first | let a long one be saved and resumed |
| survey | one question per screen, progress shown | skip logic for irrelevant questions |

A registration flow tolerates fewer fields in one step than a general form does: move to steps earlier there.

## Lead magnets by format

| Format | Shape |
| --- | --- |
| checklist | one or two pages, each line starting with a verb, ordered by process or priority, printable |
| cheat sheet | one page, built for lookup rather than reading |
| template | usable within five minutes: a "how to use" tab, example data filled in, placeholders in brackets |
| swipe file | fifteen to fifty examples, each with why it works and how to adapt it, sources named |
| ebook | density over length; one idea per chapter |
| email mini-course | three to five emails over about a week, each lesson useful alone: teach, show, exercise |
| quiz | five to ten multiple-choice questions, three to five outcomes, follow-up tailored to the outcome; the quiz is open and the result asks for the email |

These sizes are working defaults.

**Quality over count.** A good lead opens the first emails, clicks through to content or product pages, returns within a month and fits the customer profile. A poor one unsubscribes at once, opens only the delivery email, uses a throwaway address or came only for the content. Specific templates and trials bring fewer, better leads; generic ebooks, checklists and quizzes bring more, weaker ones.

**Many downloads, few trials** is first a stage mismatch: an awareness-stage checklist attracts people who are not ready to buy. Check the nurture emails and the fit with the customer profile next, then add an offer for the evaluation or decision stage, and track time to conversion by offer.

Ask whether the traffic is cold or warm before judging a rate, and when a long piece converts poorly, suspect the cost of consuming it before the page.

A blog post keeps its content open and offers an upgrade specific to that post (a template, a cheat sheet), placed at natural pauses and at the end.

## Lead magnet or free tool

A lead magnet is static, ready in hours or days and cheap to maintain. A tool is interactive, takes weeks or months, earns search traffic and shares, and can segment leads by what they entered. Build a tool only when a repeatable input produces a meaningful output and there is engineering time.

| Tool type | Design points |
| --- | --- |
| calculator | simple inputs, a transparent method, a shareable result |
| generator | output usable at once, customizable, exportable |
| analyzer or grader | a score, a comparison with a norm, actions to take |
| tester or validator | a fast, unambiguous pass or fail with how to fix it |
| resource library | searchable, easy to copy, kept current |

Classify the idea first: adjacent to the product (using it shows the value of the paid product) or pure reach (shareable, wide, converts less). Define inputs, outputs and the formula before building. When a launched tool gets no traffic, check four things: the target query and the page, distribution after launch day, whether an established free tool already owns the space, and whether the tool does anything unique. Gating: fully open earns the most use and search value; fully gated earns leads from fewer users; a basic result open with the detailed report behind an email is the usual middle. Track leads and conversions, not visits.
