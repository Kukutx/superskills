# Reading signals, building lists and matching tone

Read when deciding what a piece of account activity means, building or narrowing a prospect list, or choosing the tone for a particular situation.

## Reading signals

- **Recency first**: yesterday's signal is far stronger than one from six months ago, so every signal carries its date. One older than about three months is marked as old.
- **Clusters beat single signals.** Several pointing the same way mean more than any one.
- **No signal is a signal**: a paying account with almost no use and no contact is at risk.
- **Read by stage**: in a trial look for conversion signs, in an established customer for expansion and churn, in a prospect for intent.

| Signal | Usually means |
| --- | --- |
| more seats, deeper use of a feature | expansion |
| a spike in logins | follow up and find out why |
| falling logins, more support tickets | friction or churn risk; make contact first |
| a trial starting or about to end | time-sensitive; a decision point |
| a question in a community, a visit to a specific help page, an event sign-up | researching that capability; strong intent for the event |
| hiring for a related role, a funding round | growth and budget |
| a competitor removed from their tools | an open window; top priority |
| a champion changing jobs | a risk at the old company and an opening at the new one |

A relationship with no activity for a month has cooled and suits a light check-in; after three months it is dormant and needs a new angle. A sudden rise after a quiet period means something changed and is worth finding out before writing.

Buyer roles: champion (uses it and argues for it), economic buyer (controls the budget, may not use it), technical evaluator, end user, gatekeeper. When the material does not show the role, write "unknown"; a job title alone does not establish it.

## Ranking hooks

First by how recent (the last week or two), then by how specific (something the person did, not a general trend), then by how closely it connects to what is being sold. A hook from outside sources should be recent, publicly visible so the person knows it could be seen, and about a change: growth, a new role, a new product, a new market. "I noticed you are a customer" and an industry trend are not hooks.

When there is too little to go on, do not draft. Say that there is not enough yet and list what would help: recent activity, the background of any earlier conversation, and the specific reason for making contact now.

## Tone by situation

| Situation | Tone |
| --- | --- |
| inbound, in a trial, or active in the community | warm and helpful; open with what they did |
| cold | professional, restrained, short; open with the signal and say why now |
| reconnecting after silence | casual, low pressure; acknowledge the gap and ask for something easy |
| senior executive | formal and brief; open with the business effect, not the product |
| a champion who changed jobs | warm; congratulate first, then explore gently |

Open with "you", "your team" or what was noticed, not with "I". A phone opener has three parts that can be said naturally: who is calling and why in a few seconds, the link to their situation, and one ask. A first message on a professional network is very short, has no link or attachment and sells nothing; it only opens a door.

Never manufacture familiarity ("I've followed your company for years"), and never open with an apology for writing.

## Lists

- The least needed to start: the role, plus the industry or the company size. With less, ask one or two questions first.
- Narrow in order: companies, then the decision makers in them, then contact details for the few at the top.
- Keep two kinds of result apart and never in one table: companies with no relationship yet, which can only be filtered by outside attributes (size, industry, funding, region, tools used), and accounts already known, which can also be filtered by activity and history. If the request could mean either, ask which.
- "Companies like X": derive X's industry, size, tools, funding stage and region, and have the user confirm which of those matter before searching.
- Many results: report the count and suggest a filter. Fewer than five: say which condition to loosen. Each refinement adjusts the last filter instead of starting again.
- Sort by fit or signal strength, not alphabetically. A missing field stays empty; it is not filled with a guess.
- When the identity of a person is ambiguous, show the top three candidates and let the user choose.

## Before anything in bulk

Before a paid lookup or loading people into a sequence: show the preview (name, title, company, location) with the number and what it will cost, and wait for a yes. Remove duplicates first, and leave out anyone already in another active sequence. A personal email address is personal data: using it for outreach falls under the consent and opt-out rules the user confirms for their recipients.
